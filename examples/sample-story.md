## The Ledger of Rain

Mira stood in the doorway of her father's shuttered shop and smelled wet copper and old paper—the same smell that always meant memory. The street outside mirrored her grief: gutters choked, lamps dimming as if the city itself were holding its breath. She touched her sternum, where the ache lived, and knew she had come home to forgive a man who could no longer answer.

In the back room, a single ledger waited. Its pages chronicled debts in her father's careful hand. Mira turned them one by one, and with each entry she understood another piece of the lesson she had been refusing: love without honesty is only another kind of theft. She said this aloud, because the empty shop needed to hear it.

A neighbor, Mr. Padham, appeared with tea. They spoke of justice, of whether a child owes a parent absolution, of the philosophy of inheritance. Rain tapped the glass in thoughtful rhythm. Mira's chest tightened; cold sweat traced her spine; the lamplight thinned again. She accepted the tea. She accepted the past. She closed the ledger.

By dawn the storm had passed and the street looked rinsed and simple. Mira locked the door, lighter than she had been in years. She had chosen forgiveness, and the city—finally—seemed to approve. The moral was plain enough that she did not need to write it down: we heal when we stop running from what we already know.
